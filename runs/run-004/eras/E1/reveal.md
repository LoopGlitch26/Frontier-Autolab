# E1 historical reveal and score — hindsight review

The historical record later shows that the Web grew from an information-linking proposal into a widely adopted platform, while office networking and systems integration remained important but fragmented. This section is deliberately separated from the dated decision. CERN's 1989 proposal was a genuine signal, but it did not by itself establish adoption or a startup-sized buyer in January 1990.

Switchyard's focus on support work was buildable and plausible, but its decision to defer the linked-information opportunity leaves a major frontier signal underexplored. The proposed casebook's value would depend on repeat incidents, technician behavior, and a buyer willing to pay; no evidence in this run establishes those conditions. A company of this kind might survive as a niche support-software vendor, but broad growth is uncertain and support-heavy deployments could constrain margins.

## Playbook update

1. A repeated support burden is a candidate wedge only after recording a baseline and locating the payer.
2. Treat technical signals as options; do not infer adoption from a proposal or prototype.
3. Data collection must be authorized, low-friction, and useful to the person doing the work.
4. Cap the support surface before calling a product repeatable.
