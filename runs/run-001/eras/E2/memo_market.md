# Market & Capital: Department Memo, Era E2 (January 1996)

*Attendees: Carmen Ortiz (Head of GTM and Services Governor), Arjun Mehta (CFO), Dr. Elise Laurent (Chief Economist). Sources: the E2 world briefing, the E1 board decision and reveal, and the Playbook. No knowledge after Jan 1, 1996.*

## Transcript

**Carmen Ortiz, Head of GTM:** Every renewal call now includes "Exchange and Notes do this now." Services are at 40%, over my own cap. But the same IT managers now have an "Internet" budget line and don't know how to spend it. I hear two requests over and over: "our biggest customer demands electronic orders and the VAN charges per kilocharacter," and "marketing's Web site should take orders."

**Arjun Mehta, CFO:** The climate has flipped since our flat 1992 round. Netscape priced near $2B, UUNET, PSINet and Netcom are public, and the Fed is cutting. I propose we sell the gateway line to one of the messaging or ISP buyers circling us, then raise on a new story, not a declining one. The risk is taking hype money at hype valuations and missing the milestones.

**Dr. Elise Laurent, Chief Economist:** Windows 95 bundles TCP/IP and a browser, and Netscape nearly gives Navigator away. The client and the connection are going to zero, and the pipe is being built by players we can't outspend. What stays expensive is the **transaction**: moving an order, invoice or payment between parties who don't trust each other. Firms spend billions on VANs, fax and clerks for that. I want a per-transaction toll.

**Carmen:** Consumer Web commerce is a rounding error. The card specs aren't done, and the press is full of stolen-card stories.

**Elise:** So business-to-business goes first. Those firms already trade at volume and already pay per transaction, and the Internet cuts that price by an order of magnitude.

**Arjun:** Isn't "EDI over the Internet" just another bridge?

**Elise:** Only if we stop at translation. If we own the partner registry and the order flow, we're the network, and the protocol underneath doesn't matter to us.

## DEPARTMENT RECOMMENDATION

### Thesis A (preferred): Internet Trade Exchange
- **Chain:** Commercial Internet with SMTP/MIME and SSL as universal transport → every firm connecting while big buyers mandate electronic orders → VAN tolls and EDI complexity lock out small and mid-size suppliers → **we own a hosted trading-partner exchange (registry, routing, receipts, audit trail) over the open Internet** → data on inter-company order and invoice flows → next capability: supplier credit scoring, trade finance, and cross-company catalog procurement.
- **Why now:** NSFNET's retirement made the commercial Internet the default network. MIME encapsulation of EDI is being standardized, and VAN pricing hasn't adjusted. Our SMTP/MIME and routing skills transfer directly.
- **Wedge:** "Switchyard Trade," a flat-fee service for exchanging POs, invoices and ship notices with a large buyer, plus a Web form for suppliers with no EDI software.
- **First customer:** a tier-2 supplier to an automaker or big-box retailer that is under an EDI mandate, ideally one of our 60 accounts.
- **Makes obsolete:** per-character VAN pricing, faxed POs, and **our own gateway business**.

### Thesis B: Merchant Transaction Services
- **Chain:** Browsers plus SSL on every PC → businesses putting catalogs online → sites can't take money, compute tax or confirm orders → **we own a hosted order-and-payment back end any site plugs into** → data on cross-merchant sales and fraud → next capability: shopping comparison across merchants, and fraud scoring for card issuers.
- **Why now:** Netscape and Microsoft have made browsing mass-market, and the payment standards are converging.
- **Wedge:** hosted checkout added with a link, priced per order.
- **First customer:** a catalog retailer that wants a Web channel without building a back office.
- **Makes obsolete:** order-entry clerks and brochure-only Web sites. Competitors are Open Market, CyberCash, and the Netscape and Microsoft commerce servers.

### Playbook lessons applied
- **[E1] Bridges expire when the standards war ends.** We date our gateway's death at now and sell it while buyers still value it. In Thesis A we price the registry and settlement, never the translation.
- **[E1] Pipe, then traffic, then index.** The pipe is capital-heavy and the public index is crowded (Yahoo, Lycos, Excite, Infoseek, AltaVista). Transactions sit on traffic and carry fees.
- **[E1] Value pools where the open protocol becomes usable.** For firms, "usable" means you can do business on it.
- **[E1] Sell to existing budgets (refined).** EDI and VAN spend is the budget, and we sell it the new architecture.
- **Argued against:** reading "[E1] the next capability is the real frontier" as "build a search engine." Six funded indexes exist, so our slot is where money moves.

### Most uncertain about
1. **Bundling:** whether Microsoft, Netscape or IBM/Lotus fold commerce and EDI into servers they sell anyway, as they did with SMTP gateways.
2. **Trust:** whether buyers accept the open Internet for orders before encryption export rules and payment specs settle.
3. **Capital:** whether this IPO window lasts through an 18-month build. Arjun wants 30 months of runway raised now.
4. **Consumer pace:** whether B arrives faster than A. We propose B as a funded tripwire.
