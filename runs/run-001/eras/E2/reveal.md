# The Record: Reveal and Score, Era E2 (Jan 1996 – Dec 2001)
*Call under review: Manifest Networks. It sold the gateway line and built a hosted, receipted trading-partner exchange (EDI over MIME, plus Web forms for small suppliers), planning to grow into a trading graph and then scoring and trade finance. A Web measurement and relevance tripwire was funded at no more than 10% of burn.*

## 1. What actually happened

**Value pooled where users declared intent.** Search became the era's new franchise, and it made money through paid listings. Link analysis, not click logs, solved relevance: Kleinberg's HITS and PageRank were published in 1998. Google (incorporated September 1998) became Yahoo's results provider in June 2000 and launched AdWords that October. GoTo.com (1998, renamed Overture in 2001) invented the pay-per-click auction and syndicated it to the portals. Click-popularity ranking was real but secondary: Ask Jeeves bought Direct Hit in early 2000 for about $500M in stock. Kofi's instinct was sound, but a different signal won.

**Measurement and ad serving consolidated into a few firms.** DoubleClick (founded 1996, IPO 1998) owned ad serving, and its 1999 Abacus deal led to a privacy fight and an FTC inquiry. Media Metrix and NetRatings (both IPO 1999) shared panel measurement, and both suffered when ad spending fell in 2000–01.

**Consumer commerce came fast.** Amazon went public in 1997 and eBay in 1998, and PayPal filed for its IPO in late 2001. SET was never adopted. Checkout went to SSL forms plus gateways such as CyberSource, and CyberCash failed in 2001. Jonah and Leo had the timing right. Dev had the margins right.

**Hosted mail went free.** Microsoft bought Hotmail (launched July 1996) in 1997 for about $400M as a way to acquire users. Critical Path, which ran mail for ISPs, went public in 1999, spiked, and collapsed in 2001 in an accounting scandal. Killing Thesis 1 was correct.

**B2B boomed, went bust, and Internet EDI became a utility.** The incumbents moved as Victor predicted. Harbinger bought Premenos in 1997 and sold Internet EDI with Web forms. GE Information Services launched TPN in 1996. SBC bought Sterling Commerce and Peregrine bought Harbinger, both in 2000. XML (1998), RosettaNet, ebXML and the IETF's EDIINT AS1/AS2 work made Internet transport routine, which confirms the ~2000 bridge-death date. In 1999–2000, Ariba, Commerce One, VerticalNet and FreeMarkets reached valuations in the tens of billions, and automakers announced Covisint. The Nasdaq peaked at 5,048 on 10 March 2000 and ended 2001 near 1,950. Most B2B stocks fell more than 90%, and public exchanges closed. The B2B businesses that lasted were plain ones: EDI networks run for big buyers, and flat-fee Web-EDI for small suppliers under customer mandates.

**Infrastructure took the rent during the bubble:** Cisco (briefly the world's most valuable company), Sun, Oracle, EMC and Akamai. Then the overbuilt layers failed, including Exodus and Excite@Home in 2001.

## 2. Scorecard

| Criterion | Score | Justification |
|---|---|---|
| Frontier accuracy | **6** | Correctly saw B2B moving online, incumbent bundling, mail going free and SET's weakness. It named the real frontier (relevance and measurement) and chose away from it again. |
| Timing | **6** | Shipping in 1996–97 put it in position for the 1999 B2B wave, and the bridge-death date was accurate. Scoring and trade finance were a decade early. |
| Layer choice | **4** | A thin, low-margin layer. Each big buyer owns its own supplier relationships, so the trading graph split hub by hub and never formed a moat. Value pooled in search and paid listings, marketplaces, payments and bubble-era infrastructure. |
| Reinvention courage | **6** | Selling the gateway and killing hosted mail took real discipline. But it again picked the adjacent-skill layer and held the Web frontier at ≤10% over dissent. |
| Hindsight leakage | **7** | Mostly defensible. Points off for "usage-weighted relevance ranking" (flagged by the Red Team), "mail readable from any browser" before Hotmail, "learned fraud scoring" and a too-precise "EDI transport free by ~2000." |

**Overall era score: 52/100**

## 3. Simulated outcome
The gateway sells in Q3 1996 for about $5M, and the Series B raises $8M. Exchange 1.0 slips to Q1 1997. Kill criterion #1 is narrowly missed and waived. Suppliers join because their customers require it and send little traffic, so criterion #4 fires by 1998. Incumbent Web-EDI offerings trigger criterion #2 in 1998–99. The boom overrides both. Manifest rebrands as a "B2B trading exchange," raises about $30M in Q1 2000 at a peak valuation, grows to about 140 people, and withdraws its IPO filing after April 2000. Two layoffs in 2001 leave about 65 people, about $13M in revenue (roughly 5,000 flat-fee suppliers across 20 buyers), about $15M in cash and a heavy preference overhang. Registry and scoring revenue is under 10%, against a 30% target. The tripwire showed in 1997 that click data improves ranking, but no index would pay for it, and it was killed in 1998, the year Direct Hit and Google launched.

That path is modal, at about 45%. In about 30% of cases, a 1999–2000 stock sale nominally worth $150–400M collapses in value. In about 25%, an IPO peaks near $1B and trades below $80M by end-2001. **Verdict: survived the crash as a sub-scale Web-EDI utility, missed its liquidity window, and did not define the era.**

**Closest real-world analog:** Premenos/Harbinger's Internet EDI line, with SPS Commerce's hosted Web-EDI for retail suppliers as the survivor model. The roads not taken map to Direct Hit and Google (relevance), DoubleClick and NetRatings (measurement), Critical Path (hosted mail), and CyberSource and PayPal (checkout).

## 4. Lessons (appended to playbook.md)
1. **Gate tripwires on capability, not on incumbents' willingness to pay.** "Promote if an index will pay" asked the parties who would be displaced to buy the thing that displaces them.
2. **When every memo lands on the layer next to your current skills, suspect comfort.** If the frontier needs a team you lack, hiring that team is the thesis.
3. **Hub-and-spoke networks don't yield a neutral data moat.** The party that mandates participation owns the graph. Defensible data accrues to whoever captures voluntary intent.
4. **A bubble is a financing event, not validation of the thesis.** Raise or sell into it, hold headcount, and let pre-committed kill criteria override the market's enthusiasm.
5. **Free wins the consumer layer; money pools where intent is expressed and priced.**
