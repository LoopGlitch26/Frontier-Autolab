# World Briefing: Era E1 (as of January 1, 1990)
*Prepared by The Record. Only facts public before this date are included.*

## State of computing
- **Personal computers are mainstream in offices, rare in homes.** The IBM PC standard (MS-DOS, now 4.x) dominates business, sold by IBM and a large field of clone makers led by Compaq. IBM's PS/2 line and OS/2 (1987, jointly with Microsoft) have not taken the market the way IBM intended. Intel's 80386 is the high-end workhorse; the 80486 was announced in April 1989 and is just starting to ship. Microsoft Windows exists (2.x, Windows/386) but most PC users still work in text-mode DOS.
- **Apple's Macintosh** (1984) owns graphical computing, desktop publishing (with Adobe PostScript and Aldus PageMaker) and much of education. The Mac II line is Apple's premium product, and the Macintosh Portable appeared in September 1989. Apple is suing Microsoft (filed 1988) over the Windows look and feel.
- **Software leaders:** Lotus 1-2-3, WordPerfect, dBASE, Microsoft (fiscal 1989 revenue of about $800M), Novell NetWare for office LANs, and Oracle, Sybase and Informix in relational databases. "Client-server" is the enterprise buzzword.
- **Workstations and big iron:** Sun (the SPARCstation 1 shipped in 1989), HP/Apollo, DEC, SGI and MIPS sell RISC Unix workstations to engineers and scientists. Unix is split between OSF and Unix International. NeXT (Steve Jobs) ships an expensive object-oriented workstation. Cray and Thinking Machines (Connection Machine) lead supercomputing. IBM mainframes still carry the world's transactions. DEC is the second-largest computer company.
- **Hardware curves:** mainstream DRAM is 1Mb and 4Mb is sampling, with Japanese firms dominating memory. SEMATECH (1987) is the US response. Flash memory is newly commercial. Hard disks of 20–100MB are typical, 3.5" floppies and CD-ROM (drives are rare and expensive) handle distribution, and laser printers and fax machines are booming.

## Networks and online services
- **The Internet is a research and academic network.** It has roughly 150,000+ hosts. The NSFNET backbone (T1 since 1988) is replacing the ARPANET, which is being phased out. NSF's acceptable-use policy restricts commercial traffic. Commercial email carriers (MCI Mail, CompuServe) began exchanging mail with the Internet in 1989. The Morris worm (November 1988) led to the creation of CERT. TCP/IP competes with the government-backed OSI protocols (GOSIP).
- **Consumer online services** are small, subscription-based and billed hourly. CompuServe (~500K+ users) is the largest. Prodigy (IBM/Sears) launched nationally in 1988. GEnie is another option, and Quantum Computer Services renamed its service America Online in 1989. Thousands of hobbyist BBSes run over modems of 1200–2400 baud. France's Minitel has several million terminals.
- **Telecom:** since the 1984 AT&T breakup, the Baby Bells have been barred from information services. ISDN is heavily promoted. The first transatlantic fiber cable (TAT-8) opened in 1988. US analog cellular has roughly 3–4 million subscribers, and Motorola's MicroTAC (1989) is the smallest handset. Europe is developing the digital GSM standard for early-1990s rollout.

## Artificial intelligence and research frontier
- **AI is in retreat commercially.** The Lisp machine market has collapsed (Symbolics is struggling). Expert-system vendors have underdelivered. Japan's Fifth Generation project is widely seen as falling short.
- **Neural networks are reviving in research.** This follows the backpropagation work popularized by Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams (1986) and the PDP volumes. Bell Labs has shown handwritten ZIP code recognition, and CMU's ALVINN steers a vehicle with a small network. Statistical and HMM-based speech recognition (IBM, CMU's Sphinx) is advancing. The chess machine Deep Thought beat a grandmaster in 1988 but lost to Kasparov in October 1989.
- **Other whispers:** hypertext (HyperCard, Xanadu, the ACM Hypertext conferences); virtual reality (VPL Research, Autodesk's "Cyberspace" project); pen computing (GO Corp, the GRiDPad); massively parallel machines; high-temperature superconductors (1986–87); nanotechnology (Drexler's *Engines of Creation*); the GNU project and the GPL (1989); object-oriented programming (C++ 2.0, Smalltalk); and the planned Human Genome Project. The cold-fusion claims of March 1989 have been largely discredited.

## Consumer electronics and media
Nintendo dominates home video games with the NES and launched the handheld Game Boy in 1989. Sega released the Genesis in the US in 1989. Other consumer staples are the VCR, cable TV, CD audio and the camcorder. HDTV standards are being debated, and Japan is the early leader.

## Capital climate
- The October 1987 crash still weighs on markets. US venture fundraising has fallen from its mid-1980s peak, and the IPO window for tech is narrow. The savings-and-loan crisis triggered the FIRREA bailout (August 1989). The junk-bond market is under stress after Drexel Burnham's 1988 guilty plea. US interest rates are high (prime around 10–11%).
- Japan's asset boom continues, with the Nikkei at record highs at year-end 1989. Japanese firms are major investors in US assets and a competitive threat in semiconductors and electronics.
- A small software company can be started on modest seed money. Hardware and semiconductors require tens of millions of dollars.

## Society and regulation
- **Geopolitics:** the Berlin Wall fell on November 9, 1989, and communist governments across Eastern Europe are collapsing. China suppressed the Tiananmen protests in June 1989. The EC is working toward a single market by 1992. US–Japan trade friction is a major political theme.
- **Law and policy:** relevant US law includes the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (1986) and the Electronic Communications Privacy Act (1986). Strong cryptography faces export controls, and RSA is patented. Software copyright and "look and feel" cases (Lotus, Apple) are unresolved.
- **Public perception:** computing is seen as a productivity tool for business and a hobby for enthusiasts. Hackers and viruses are rising public concerns, as popularized by *The Cuckoo's Egg* (1989).
